\section{Introduction}

Deep neural networks (DNNs) trained with backpropagation have become the dominant computational models of the ventral visual stream \citep{yamins2016}. A well-established finding is that the hierarchy of features learned by DNNs mirrors the hierarchy of representations from V1 through IT cortex, as quantified by representational similarity analysis (RSA) and related metrics \citep{kriegeskorte2008, schrimpf2020}. This correspondence has been interpreted as evidence that the objectives and inductive biases of deep learning capture something fundamental about visual processing.

Yet backpropagation is neurobiologically implausible in at least three well-known respects: it requires symmetric forward and feedback weights (the weight transport problem), it relies on a global error signal propagated backward through the network, and the forward and backward passes must be temporally separated \citep{crick1989, lillicrap2020}. These observations have motivated work on biologically plausible alternatives, including feedback alignment \citep{lillicrap2016}, predictive coding \citep{rao1999, whittington2017}, and spike-timing-dependent plasticity \citep{bi1998}.

A key question is: does the choice of learning rule matter for brain alignment? If alignment arises from the architecture and objective rather than the weight-update rule, then alternative rules---or even no training at all---should yield comparable alignment. Prior work has compared individual learning rules to brain data \citep{schrimpf2020}, but to our knowledge no study has systematically compared all four rules together with an untrained random-weights baseline on the same architecture, stimuli, and RSA pipeline.

We address this gap with five conditions: BP, FA, PC, STDP, and a random-weights (untrained) baseline, all applied to identical CNNs trained on CIFAR-10, with layer-wise RDMs compared to human fMRI from the THINGS-fMRI dataset.

Our main contributions are: (1) a head-to-head RSA comparison of four learning rules plus a random baseline at $224\times224$ resolution, averaged across 5 seeds; (2) the finding that\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{V1/V2 alignment is architecture-driven, with }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{, at $224$\,px evaluation, }\DIFaddend the untrained CNN \DIFdelbegin \emph{\DIFdel{exceeding}} %DIFAUXCMD
\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{exceeds }\DIFaddend BP at V1 \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{($\Delta\rho = +0.044$, $p < 0.001$)}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{and V2 (V1: $\Delta\rho = \dps{rnd}{bp}{V1}$, 95\% CI $\dpsci{rnd}{bp}{V1}$), while at V1 the two do not differ at the $32$\,px training resolution}\DIFaddend ; (3) the result that at IT, \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{all trained rules converge and are statistically indistinguishable from each other}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{no two conditions can be distinguished at the low reliability of the IT data}\DIFaddend ; (4) the finding that \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{only LOC shows a reliable learning effect, with BP being the sole rule to significantly exceed the random baseline}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{LOC is the only area where trained rules exceed the untrained baseline: BP, PC and STDP do so while the trained rules do not differ from each other}\DIFaddend ; and (5) the \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{consistent }\DIFdelend finding that FA produces the lowest brain alignment at V1\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{, V2, and LOC}\DIFdelend .

\section{Related Work}

\textbf{RSA as a model evaluation tool.} \citet{kriegeskorte2008} introduced RSA as a framework for comparing representations across measurement modalities. The core idea is to represent each system's response to a stimulus set as a pairwise dissimilarity matrix (RDM) and to assess model--brain alignment as the rank correlation between RDMs. RSA has since become a standard tool for comparing DNNs to neural data \citep{yamins2016, schrimpf2020}.

\textbf{DNNs and the visual hierarchy.} Task-optimized DNNs reproduce the representational hierarchy of the ventral visual stream, with early layers matching V1 and deeper layers matching IT \citep{yamins2016}. The Brain-Score benchmark \citep{schrimpf2020} systematizes this evaluation across architectures and datasets. Most high-scoring models are trained with standard BP on ImageNet. Importantly, \citet{schrimpf2020} noted that untrained networks retain some brain alignment, but did not systematically compare this against multiple learning rules.

\textbf{Biologically plausible learning rules.} Feedback alignment \citep{lillicrap2016} replaces the transpose weight matrix in the backward pass with fixed random matrices, removing the weight transport problem. \citet{lillicrap2016} showed that FA still allows learning in fully-connected networks, though its effectiveness in convolutional architectures is less established. Predictive coding \citep{rao1999} frames inference as hierarchical prediction-error minimization; \citet{whittington2017} showed that PC implements an approximation to BP under certain conditions, with learning driven entirely by local Hebbian updates. Spike-timing-dependent plasticity \citep{bi1998} updates synaptic weights based on the relative timing of pre- and postsynaptic spikes, and is one of the best-characterized forms of synaptic plasticity in mammalian cortex. Each of these rules has been studied individually in the context of neural representation learning, but direct comparisons on identical architectures and evaluation pipelines are rare.

\textbf{Random networks and early visual cortex.} \citet{saxe2011} demonstrated that random-weight networks can exhibit non-trivial visual structure. Concurrently with our work, \citet{truzzi2025} showed that random-weight CNNs predict early visual cortex responses when model complexity is sufficient. Our work extends these observations by systematically comparing four distinct learning rules against the random baseline \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{, and by demonstrating that the architecture-dominance effect is not limited to V1: at IT, all rules---including random---converge with no significant pairwise differences}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{across the visual hierarchy}\DIFaddend .

\textbf{Sparse coding and V1.} \citet{olshausen1996} showed that unsupervised learning of a sparse code for natural images produces receptive fields resembling V1 simple cells, establishing that correlation-based learning from image statistics suffices to produce V1-like representations without supervised objectives.

\subsection{Learning Rules}

\textbf{Random Weights} serves as the architectural baseline. The network is initialized with \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{He-normal weights }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{PyTorch's default }\texttt{\DIFadd{kaiming\_uniform\_}} \DIFaddend and never trained, isolating the contribution of the convolutional architecture (filters, ReLU, pooling) to brain alignment. \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{FA and STDP initialize with }\texttt{\DIFadd{kaiming\_normal\_}}\DIFadd{, whose standard deviation is larger by a factor of about $\NV{init.ratio}$. The untrained network therefore shares its initialization with BP and PC and is their matched control; for FA and STDP, comparisons with the untrained network also include the difference in initialization scale.
}\DIFaddend 

\textbf{Backpropagation (BP)} serves as the supervised baseline: $\Delta W_l = -\eta \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial W_l}$.

\textbf{Feedback Alignment (FA)} \citep{lillicrap2016} replaces $\mathbf{W}_l^\top$ in the backward pass with a fixed random matrix $\mathbf{B}_l$:
\begin{equation}
\Delta W_l = \delta_l^{\text{FA}} \mathbf{h}_{l-1}^\top, \quad \delta_l^{\text{FA}} = f'(z_l) \odot (\mathbf{B}_{l+1} \delta_{l+1}^{\text{FA}}).
\end{equation}

\textbf{Predictive Coding (PC)} \citep{rao1999, whittington2017} minimizes a hierarchical prediction-error energy $F = \sum_l \|\varepsilon_l\|^2$ through iterative inference ($T_{\text{inf}} = 10$ steps, inference rate $\alpha = 0.02$):
\begin{equation}
\Delta W_l \propto \varepsilon_l \mathbf{x}_{l-1}^\top.
\end{equation}
Prediction weights $\mathbf{P}_l$ (implemented as transposed convolutions) generate top-down predictions; prediction errors $\varepsilon_l = r_l - \hat{r}_l$ drive both inference dynamics (updating representations $r_l$) and weight learning (weight rate $\eta_W = 10^{-4}$). As with STDP, only convolutional weights are updated via PC; the fully connected readout layers are trained with backpropagation.

\textbf{STDP} \citep{bi1998} converts activations to Poisson spike trains ($T = 10$ timesteps) and uses first-spike timing to update convolutional weights:
\begin{equation}
\Delta w = \begin{cases} A_+ e^{-\Delta t / \tau_+} & \Delta t > 0 \text{ (LTP)} \\ -A_- e^{\Delta t / \tau_-} & \Delta t < 0 \text{ (LTD)} \end{cases}
\end{equation}
with $\tau_+ = \tau_- = 20$\,ms, $A_+ = A_- = 0.003$, $\text{lr} = 5 \times 10^{-4}$. Only the convolutional layers (Conv1--Conv3) are updated via STDP; the fully connected layers (FC1, FC2) are trained with backpropagation to provide a supervised readout, analogous to a linear probe on STDP-learned features. The \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{63\% CIFAR-10 accuracy }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{STDP test accuracy ($\NV{acc.stdp}\,\%$) }\DIFaddend therefore reflects STDP feature quality evaluated through a BP-trained classifier, not end-to-end STDP learning.

\subsection{RSA Pipeline}

\textbf{Feature extraction.} For each layer, activations were extracted on the 720 THINGS stimuli (resized to $224\times224$, standard ResNet input resolution). Convolutional activations were spatially averaged (global average pooling). All results are averaged across 5 random seeds per learning rule\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{; error bars in figures reflect seed variability (mean $\pm$ std)}\DIFdelend .

\textbf{RDM construction.} Model RDMs were computed as $1 - r$ (correlation distance).
\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{Brain RDMs were averaged across subjects to produce a single mean-brain RDM per ROI.
}\DIFdelend 

\textbf{RSA score.} Model--brain alignment \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{was quantified as }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{is }\DIFaddend Spearman's $\rho$ between the upper triangles of \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{model and mean-brain RDMs}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{a model RDM and a subject RDM, restricted to the stimulus pairs defined below. The main convention ($\rho_{\text{subj}}$) correlates the model RDM with each subject's RDM and averages over the three subjects. The convention of earlier versions ($\rho_{\text{mean}}$) correlates the model RDM with the average of the three subject RDMs; it is reported alongside}\DIFaddend .

\textbf{\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{Bootstrap confidence intervals}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{Stimulus pairs (cross-run)}\DIFaddend .} \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{95\% CIs were estimated by bootstrap resampling ($N = 10{,}000$)of the stimulus pairs from the mean-brain RDM comparison. This sample size is sufficient for stable CI estimation at the correlation values observed here. }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{Each subject saw the 720 stimuli in ten runs, and sub-01 and sub-02 saw them in identical order. Responses within a run can share run-specific components (baseline drifts, run-wise noise), so for two subjects with the same order the dissimilarities of same-run pairs share a component that has nothing to do with the stimuli. This inflated the agreement between sub-01 and sub-02 (at LOC $\NV{agree.0102.LOC.all}$ on all pairs vs.\ $\NV{agree.0102.LOC.cr}$ on cross-run pairs, while sub-01 vs.\ sub-03 is $\NV{agree.0103.LOC.all}$ and $\NV{agree.0103.LOC.cr}$) and thereby the lower bound at LOC and IT (appendix, Table~\ref{tab:sens}). We therefore use only stimulus pairs that lie in different runs for all three subjects: $\NV{pairs.cr}$ of $\NV{pairs.total}$ pairs; $\NV{pairs.excl.pct}\,\%$ are excluded. Within a run, dissimilarity also depends on the distance between trials (Spearman correlation between dissimilarity and lag $\NV{lag.rho.min}$ to $\NV{lag.rho.max}$); at LOC and IT, neighbouring trials are on average anticorrelated (mean dissimilarity at lag 1 up to $\NV{lag.one.max}$). This would be consistent with collinearity between the overlapping hemodynamic responses of neighbouring trials in single-trial estimates, which is a further reason to exclude these pairs; we did not test this explanation. Excluding same-run pairs follows the same logic as run-wise cross-validation of dissimilarities, which keeps noise shared within a run out of the dissimilarity estimates \mbox{%DIFAUXCMD
\citep{walther2016}}\hskip0pt%DIFAUXCMD
.
}\DIFaddend 

\textbf{Noise ceiling.} \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{Upper and lower bounds were estimated using split-half reliability corrected by the Spearman--Brown formula \mbox{%DIFAUXCMD
\citep{kriegeskorte2008}}\hskip0pt%DIFAUXCMD
}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{The lower bound follows \mbox{%DIFAUXCMD
\citet{nili2014}}\hskip0pt%DIFAUXCMD
: each subject's RDM is correlated with the average RDM of the other two subjects, and the three correlations are averaged. It estimates how well a model that captures the structure shared across subjects would score, and is used here as a floor: a model below it leaves reliable shared structure unexplained. The upper bound (correlation of each subject with the average of all three, including itself) is not informative with three subjects: under a permutation null in which stimulus labels are shuffled independently per subject ($\NV{nperm}$ permutations), the upper bound stays large (V1: $\NV{ubnull.cr.V1.mean}$ against an observed $\NV{ub.cr.V1}$), because each subject contributes a third of the average it is compared with. It is therefore not shown in the figures. Under the same null the lower bound is $\NV{lbnull.cr.V1.mean} \pm \NV{lbnull.cr.V1.sd}$ (V1), so the observed lower bounds are far from chance at every ROI}\DIFaddend .

\section{Discussion}

\textbf{Architecture as the primary driver of early alignment.} Our most striking finding is that an untrained CNN \emph{exceeds} backpropagation at V1 (\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{$\rho = 0.076$ }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{$\rho = \rs{rnd}{V1}$ }\DIFaddend vs.\ \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{$0.034$, $\Delta\rho = +0.044$, $p < 0.001$}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{$\rs{bp}{V1}$, $\Delta\rho = \dps{rnd}{bp}{V1}$ $\dpsci{rnd}{bp}{V1}$}\DIFaddend ). This goes beyond the observation of \citet{saxe2011} that random networks have non-trivial visual structure: in our setup, \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{at $224$\,px evaluation, }\DIFaddend the convolutional inductive biases (local connectivity, ReLU, pooling) are not merely comparable to BP but produce higher early visual alignment. \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{This suggests that , at least for small CNNs trained on CIFAR-10, backpropagation on a classification objective moves }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{The advantage is not evidence that training degrades }\DIFaddend early-layer \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{representations }\emph{\DIFdel{away}} %DIFAUXCMD
\DIFdel{from V1-like structure. Whether this }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{structure: when the models are evaluated at their $32$\,px training resolution, BP and the untrained network do not differ at V1 ($\rho_{\text{subj}} = \NV{res.bp.32}$ vs.\ $\NV{res.rnd.32}$; $\Delta\rho = \NV{res.gap.32}$, 95\% CI $[\NV{res.gap.32.lo},\NV{res.gap.32.hi}]$, model set of the resolution study), and the untrained network's lead at $224$\,px arises from the difference between training and evaluation resolution (\newid). Whether the same }\DIFaddend holds at larger scales (e.g., ResNet-50 on ImageNet) remains to be tested.
\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{Among trained rules, STDP ($\rho = 0.064$) and PC ($\rho = 0.056$)preserve more V1-like structure than BP, consistent with the idea that local unsupervised learning retains more of the architectural inductive bias \mbox{%DIFAUXCMD

\textbf{\DIFadd{On the untrained baseline.}} \DIFadd{The untrained condition's batch-normalisation layers hold their initialised statistics, so in evaluation mode they act as the identity, whereas every trained condition carries statistics accumulated during training. A $2\times2$ calibration control varying the calibration set and the calibration resolution, with the convolutional weights held bit-identical, indicates that this does not produce the result: with statistics calibrated at the evaluation resolution the untrained network still exceeds backpropagation at V1 by $\NV{cal.B}$ (CIFAR-10 statistics) or $\NV{cal.D}$ (statistics from a disjoint pool of THINGS images), $\NV{cal.B.n}$ and $\NV{cal.D.n}$ seeds (original analysis: all pairs, mean-RDM convention). The result is, however, strongly dependent on the evaluation resolution, which this paper holds fixed at $224$\,px; see \newid}\DIFaddend .

\textbf{\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{Learning rules converge }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{Few reliable differences }\DIFaddend at higher areas.} At LOC, \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{only BPsignificantly exceeds }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{BP, PC and STDP exceed }\DIFaddend the random baseline, \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{but }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{FA is borderline, and the }\DIFaddend differences among trained rules are small and \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{non-significant after FDR correction}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{not significant}\DIFaddend . At IT, \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{all five conditions converge completely. This convergence }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{no two conditions can be distinguished. Prior work with larger models suggests that BP-trained networks have a systematic IT advantage \citep{schrimpf2020}; whether this advantage reflects the learning rule or the additional model capacity remains an open question. \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{We note that the noise ceiling at IT is also the lowest of all ROIs (upper bound : 0.07), leaving limited room for any condition to differentiate --- it is possible that true differences }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{The low reliability of the IT data (lower bound $\lb{IT}$, with every condition below it) also leaves little room for differences to be resolved with three subjects; true differences may }\DIFaddend exist but are \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{unresolvable given the fMRI signal quality at this ROI and sample size ($N = 3$)}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{not resolvable here}\DIFaddend .

\textbf{\DIFdel{FA consistently lowest.}} %DIFAUXCMD
\DIFdelend FA produces the lowest alignment \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{among trained rules }\DIFdelend at V1, \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{V2, and LOC, }\DIFdelend significantly below all other conditions\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{at V1/V2 ($p < 0.001$), despite achieving 39\% task }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{, despite reaching $\NV{acc.fa}\,\%$ test }\DIFaddend accuracy. This result may be specific to CNNs, where spatial structure in feature maps interacts poorly with random feedback filters \citep{lillicrap2016}.

\textbf{Dissociation between task performance and brain alignment.} Task accuracy and brain alignment are dissociated \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{throughout the hierarchy }\DIFdelend in our small-scale setting. At V1, the untrained baseline (\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{10\% }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{$\NV{acc.rnd}\,\%$ test }\DIFaddend accuracy) exceeds BP (\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{82\% accuracy}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{$\NV{acc.bp}\,\%$}\DIFaddend ), and \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{STDP (63\%) and PC (57\%) both outperform BP despite lower task performance. At IT, all rules converge regardless of accuracy}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{at IT, conditions with test accuracies between $\NV{acc.rnd}\,\%$ and $\NV{acc.bp}\,\%$ cannot be distinguished}\DIFaddend . This suggests that, at least for small CNNs trained on CIFAR-10, the relevant axis is not ``how well does it classify'' but ``what representational structure does the objective induce at each layer.'' Whether this dissociation persists at ImageNet scale, where task-optimized models achieve substantially higher Brain-Score alignment \citep{schrimpf2020}, remains an open question.

\textbf{Implications.} Our results carry two concrete methodological messages. For computational neuroscience: studies comparing trained models to brain data must always include an untrained baseline with the same architecture. Without this control, architecture effects are confounded with learning effects---our data show this confound can be essentially complete at V1. \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{For machine learning: the inductive biases of the convolutional architecture (local connectivity, weight sharing, ReLU, pooling) are more important than the weight-update rule for }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{The same control must be run at the training resolution: the untrained network's lead over BP at V1 is present at $224$\,px evaluation but absent at the $32$\,px training resolution; it arises from the mismatch between training and evaluation resolution, not from training per se degrading }\DIFaddend early-layer \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{feature representations. When designing architectures for brain-inspired AI, the structural priors deserve as much attention as the learning algorithm}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{structure (\newid). A second message comes from the revision itself: when subjects share a presentation order, same-run stimulus pairs can inflate between-subject agreement and the noise ceiling, and should be excluded or modelled}\DIFaddend .

\subsection{Limitations}

Several limitations bound our conclusions. (1)~All models were trained on CIFAR-10 using a small custom CNN, far below the scale at which ResNet-50 or ViT models achieve competitive Brain-Score results. The small architecture limits representational capacity and may \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{explain the IT convergence}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{contribute to the absence of differences at IT}\DIFaddend . (2)~We have only 3 subjects, with sub-03 showing consistently weak signal; our core findings are driven by sub-01 and sub-02. \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{With three subjects only the lower noise bound is informative. }\DIFaddend Replication on a larger dataset (e.g., Natural Scenes Dataset, $N = 8$) would strengthen generalizability. (3)~STDP was applied to static images via Poisson spike trains, discarding the temporal dynamics that STDP was designed to exploit. (4)~The best-layer-per-ROI analysis reveals that FC1 sometimes produces higher V1 alignment than Conv1, complicating the anatomical mapping assumption. (5)~Gabor-peakedness scores show no significant differences across learning rules, but this metric captures only a coarse proxy for V1-like filter structure. (6)~\DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{Bootstrap CIs resample stimulus pairsrather than stimuli, which may underestimate uncertainty}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{Restricting the analysis to cross-run pairs removes $\NV{pairs.excl.pct}\,\%$ of the stimulus pairs; it removes the shared run component, but the sensitivity analyses (appendix) show that the main conclusions do not depend on this choice}\DIFaddend . (7)~We use a single dataset (THINGS-fMRI); cross-dataset replication would strengthen all claims. (8)~Models were trained at $32\times32$ resolution but evaluated at $224\times224$; \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{random filters are approximately scale-invariant while trained filters are optimized for $32\times32$ statistics, which may partly contribute to the random }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{the size of the untrained }\DIFaddend baseline's \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{advantage at }\DIFdelend V1 \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{advantage depends strongly on the evaluation resolution, which is quantified in \newid}\DIFaddend .

\section{Conclusion}

We presented a systematic RSA comparison of four learning rules plus an untrained baseline at $224\times224$ resolution, averaged across 5 seeds, against the THINGS-fMRI benchmark. In our small-scale setup, the learning rule has little effect on cortical alignment: architecture dominates early visual representations (V1/V2), where the untrained baseline exceeds all trained rules \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{and, at V1, is the only condition that reaches the lower noise bound. This advantage holds at the $224$\,px evaluation resolution; in the model set of the resolution study (same architecture and training, 5 seeds) the V1 gap is $\Delta\rho = \NV{res.gap.224}$ (95\% CI $[\NV{res.gap.224.lo},\NV{res.gap.224.hi}]$) at $224$\,px, and when the models are evaluated at their $32$\,px training resolution it vanishes ($\Delta\rho = \NV{res.gap.32}$}\DIFaddend , \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{and all rules converge at higher areas (IT)}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{95\% CI $[\NV{res.gap.32.lo},\NV{res.gap.32.hi}]$): BP and the untrained network do not differ at V1, and the untrained network's lead at $224$\,px arises from the difference between training and evaluation resolution (\newid). At LOC}\DIFaddend , \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{with no reliable pairwise differences---though this convergence may partly reflect model capacity limitations. Only LOCshows a selective learning effect, where BP alone reliably exceeds the random baseline. Among trained rules , PC and STDP preserve more V1-like structure than BP, suggesting that local unsupervised learning retains architectural inductive biases that global error signals may disrupt}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{three of the four trained rules exceed the untrained baseline (FA borderline) without differing from each other; at IT, no two conditions can be distinguished at the low reliability of the data---though this may also reflect model capacity limitations}\DIFaddend . FA consistently produces the lowest alignment \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{despite meaningful task accuracy }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{at V1 despite above-chance accuracy ($\NV{acc.fa}\,\%$)}\DIFaddend . These results suggest that, at least at this scale, \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{the search for biologically plausible learning rules should focus on preserving architectural inductive biases }\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{learning-rule comparisons }\DIFaddend at early areas \DIFdelbegin \DIFdel{, rather than optimizing for task performance}\DIFdelend \DIFaddbegin \DIFadd{are dominated by the architecture and by the relation between training and evaluation resolution, and should report both}\DIFaddend . Whether these findings generalize to larger architectures trained on richer datasets remains an important open question.
