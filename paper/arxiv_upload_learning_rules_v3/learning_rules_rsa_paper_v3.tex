\noindent\textbf{Revised version (v4).} This version replaces the noise ceiling, restricts all
analyses to stimulus pairs that were presented in different fMRI runs, and uses the repaired
evaluation of all five conditions; see the revision note below.

\medskip

A central question in computational neuroscience is whether the learning rule used to train a neural network determines how well its internal representations align with those of the human visual cortex. We present a systematic comparison of four learning rules---backpropagation (BP), feedback alignment (FA), predictive coding (PC), and spike-timing-dependent plasticity (STDP)---applied to identical convolutional architectures and evaluated against human fMRI data from the THINGS-fMRI dataset (720 stimuli, 3 subjects) using Representational Similarity Analysis (RSA). All models process stimuli at $224\times224$ resolution; results are averaged across 5 random seeds, and uncertainty is estimated by resampling stimuli. Crucially, we include an untrained random-weights baseline that isolates the contribution of architecture. At V1, the untrained baseline \emph{exceeds} backpropagation ($\rho = \rs{rnd}{V1}$ vs.\ $\rho = \rs{bp}{V1}$; $\Delta\rho = \dps{rnd}{bp}{V1}$, 95\% CI $\dpsci{rnd}{bp}{V1}$), and the same holds at V2. This advantage depends on the evaluation resolution: the models are trained at $32$\,px and evaluated at $224$\,px. In the 5-seed model set of the resolution study (same architecture and training; gap $\NV{res.gap.224.3}$ at $224$\,px), the gap vanishes when the models are evaluated at $32$\,px ($\Delta\rho = \NV{res.gap.32.3}$, 95\% CI $[\NV{res.gap.32.lo.3}, \NV{res.gap.32.hi.3}]$; \newid). At LOC, BP, PC and STDP exceed the random baseline, FA is borderline, and the trained rules do not differ from each other. At IT, no two conditions can be distinguished; the reliability of the IT data is low (leave-one-subject-out lower bound $\lb{IT}$). FA produces the lowest alignment at V1. Only the untrained network reaches the lower noise bound at V1; at V2 it and STDP are not distinguishable from the bound, and every condition lies below it at LOC and IT. These results indicate that, at the $224$\,px evaluation resolution used here, early visual alignment is driven more by the architecture than by the learning rule, and that learning-rule differences are resolvable only at intermediate areas.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} representational similarity analysis, visual cortex, biologically plausible learning, random baseline, STDP, feedback alignment, predictive coding, fMRI, THINGS
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Deep neural networks (DNNs) trained with backpropagation have become the dominant computational models of the ventral visual stream \citep{yamins2016}. A well-established finding is that the hierarchy of features learned by DNNs mirrors the hierarchy of representations from V1 through IT cortex, as quantified by representational similarity analysis (RSA) and related metrics \citep{kriegeskorte2008, schrimpf2020}. This correspondence has been interpreted as evidence that the objectives and inductive biases of deep learning capture something fundamental about visual processing.

Yet backpropagation is neurobiologically implausible in at least three well-known respects: it requires symmetric forward and feedback weights (the weight transport problem), it relies on a global error signal propagated backward through the network, and the forward and backward passes must be temporally separated \citep{crick1989, lillicrap2020}. These observations have motivated work on biologically plausible alternatives, including feedback alignment \citep{lillicrap2016}, predictive coding \citep{rao1999, whittington2017}, and spike-timing-dependent plasticity \citep{bi1998}.

A key question is: does the choice of learning rule matter for brain alignment? If alignment arises from the architecture and objective rather than the weight-update rule, then alternative rules---or even no training at all---should yield comparable alignment. Prior work has compared individual learning rules to brain data \citep{schrimpf2020}, but to our knowledge no study has systematically compared all four rules together with an untrained random-weights baseline on the same architecture, stimuli, and RSA pipeline.

We address this gap with five conditions: BP, FA, PC, STDP, and a random-weights (untrained) baseline, all applied to identical CNNs trained on CIFAR-10, with layer-wise RDMs compared to human fMRI from the THINGS-fMRI dataset.

Our main contributions are: (1) a head-to-head RSA comparison of four learning rules plus a random baseline at $224\times224$ resolution, averaged across 5 seeds; (2) the finding that, at $224$\,px evaluation, the untrained CNN exceeds BP at V1 and V2 (V1: $\Delta\rho = \dps{rnd}{bp}{V1}$, 95\% CI $\dpsci{rnd}{bp}{V1}$), while at V1 the two do not differ at the $32$\,px training resolution; (3) the result that at IT, no two conditions can be distinguished at the low reliability of the IT data; (4) the finding that LOC is the only area where trained rules exceed the untrained baseline: BP, PC and STDP do so while the trained rules do not differ from each other; and (5) the finding that FA produces the lowest brain alignment at V1.

\section{Related Work}

\textbf{RSA as a model evaluation tool.} \citet{kriegeskorte2008} introduced RSA as a framework for comparing representations across measurement modalities. The core idea is to represent each system's response to a stimulus set as a pairwise dissimilarity matrix (RDM) and to assess model--brain alignment as the rank correlation between RDMs. RSA has since become a standard tool for comparing DNNs to neural data \citep{yamins2016, schrimpf2020}.

\textbf{DNNs and the visual hierarchy.} Task-optimized DNNs reproduce the representational hierarchy of the ventral visual stream, with early layers matching V1 and deeper layers matching IT \citep{yamins2016}. The Brain-Score benchmark \citep{schrimpf2020} systematizes this evaluation across architectures and datasets. Most high-scoring models are trained with standard BP on ImageNet. Importantly, \citet{schrimpf2020} noted that untrained networks retain some brain alignment, but did not systematically compare this against multiple learning rules.

\textbf{Biologically plausible learning rules.} Feedback alignment \citep{lillicrap2016} replaces the transpose weight matrix in the backward pass with fixed random matrices, removing the weight transport problem. \citet{lillicrap2016} showed that FA still allows learning in fully-connected networks, though its effectiveness in convolutional architectures is less established. Predictive coding \citep{rao1999} frames inference as hierarchical prediction-error minimization; \citet{whittington2017} showed that PC implements an approximation to BP under certain conditions, with learning driven entirely by local Hebbian updates. Spike-timing-dependent plasticity \citep{bi1998} updates synaptic weights based on the relative timing of pre- and postsynaptic spikes, and is one of the best-characterized forms of synaptic plasticity in mammalian cortex. Each of these rules has been studied individually in the context of neural representation learning, but direct comparisons on identical architectures and evaluation pipelines are rare.

\textbf{Random networks and early visual cortex.} \citet{saxe2011} demonstrated that random-weight networks can exhibit non-trivial visual structure. Concurrently with our work, \citet{truzzi2025} showed that random-weight CNNs predict early visual cortex responses when model complexity is sufficient. Our work extends these observations by systematically comparing four distinct learning rules against the random baseline across the visual hierarchy.

\textbf{Sparse coding and V1.} \citet{olshausen1996} showed that unsupervised learning of a sparse code for natural images produces receptive fields resembling V1 simple cells, establishing that correlation-based learning from image statistics suffices to produce V1-like representations without supervised objectives.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Dataset}

We used the THINGS-fMRI dataset, part of THINGS-data \citep{hebart2023}: 3T fMRI responses (Siemens Magnetom Prisma) of 3 subjects to 8{,}740 unique images of 720 object concepts from the THINGS database \citep{hebart2019}, recorded in 12 main-experiment sessions of 10 runs each. We selected 720 stimuli (one image per concept, each presented once). fMRI responses were available for 3 subjects across 4 regions of interest (ROIs): V1, V2, LOC, and IT. For each subject and ROI, single-trial responses were z-scored per voxel across all trials, and a subject RDM was computed as the correlation distance between the response patterns of the 720 stimuli.

\subsection{Model Architecture}

All five conditions used an identical CNN architecture consisting of three convolutional layers (Conv1--Conv3, each followed by BatchNorm, ReLU, and $2\times2$ max-pooling) and two fully connected layers (FC1, FC2). The output layer uses softmax for 10-class classification on CIFAR-10. Training used 8,000 CIFAR-10 samples for 40 epochs at native $32\times32$ resolution. For RSA evaluation, THINGS stimuli were resized to $224\times224$ and passed through the same trained (or untrained) network; this is standard practice in model--brain comparisons where training and evaluation datasets differ \citep{schrimpf2020}. All results are from the five-seed re-run with the repaired evaluation mode (Revision note, item iii); the original model set is reported as a sensitivity column in the appendix.

The fixed layer-to-ROI mapping is Conv1$\to$V1, Conv1$\to$V2, Conv3$\to$LOC, FC1$\to$IT. V1 and V2 are both mapped to Conv1; Conv2 is not used in the fixed mapping (its values are given in the best-layer analysis, Table~\ref{tab:bestlayer}).

\subsection{Learning Rules}

\textbf{Random Weights} serves as the architectural baseline. The network is initialized with PyTorch's default \texttt{kaiming\_uniform\_} and never trained, isolating the contribution of the convolutional architecture (filters, ReLU, pooling) to brain alignment. FA and STDP initialize with \texttt{kaiming\_normal\_}, whose standard deviation is larger by a factor of about $\NV{init.ratio}$. The untrained network therefore shares its initialization with BP and PC and is their matched control; for FA and STDP, comparisons with the untrained network also include the difference in initialization scale.

\textbf{Backpropagation (BP)} serves as the supervised baseline: $\Delta W_l = -\eta \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial W_l}$.

\textbf{Feedback Alignment (FA)} \citep{lillicrap2016} replaces $\mathbf{W}_l^\top$ in the backward pass with a fixed random matrix $\mathbf{B}_l$:
\begin{equation}
\Delta W_l = \delta_l^{\text{FA}} \mathbf{h}_{l-1}^\top, \quad \delta_l^{\text{FA}} = f'(z_l) \odot (\mathbf{B}_{l+1} \delta_{l+1}^{\text{FA}}).
\end{equation}

\textbf{Predictive Coding (PC)} \citep{rao1999, whittington2017} minimizes a hierarchical prediction-error energy $F = \sum_l \|\varepsilon_l\|^2$ through iterative inference ($T_{\text{inf}} = 10$ steps, inference rate $\alpha = 0.02$):
\begin{equation}
\Delta W_l \propto \varepsilon_l \mathbf{x}_{l-1}^\top.
\end{equation}
Prediction weights $\mathbf{P}_l$ (implemented as transposed convolutions) generate top-down predictions; prediction errors $\varepsilon_l = r_l - \hat{r}_l$ drive both inference dynamics (updating representations $r_l$) and weight learning (weight rate $\eta_W = 10^{-4}$). As with STDP, only convolutional weights are updated via PC; the fully connected readout layers are trained with backpropagation.

\textbf{STDP} \citep{bi1998} converts activations to Poisson spike trains ($T = 10$ timesteps) and uses first-spike timing to update convolutional weights:
\begin{equation}
\Delta w = \begin{cases} A_+ e^{-\Delta t / \tau_+} & \Delta t > 0 \text{ (LTP)} \\ -A_- e^{\Delta t / \tau_-} & \Delta t < 0 \text{ (LTD)} \end{cases}
\end{equation}
with $\tau_+ = \tau_- = 20$\,ms, $A_+ = A_- = 0.003$, $\text{lr} = 5 \times 10^{-4}$. Only the convolutional layers (Conv1--Conv3) are updated via STDP; the fully connected layers (FC1, FC2) are trained with backpropagation to provide a supervised readout, analogous to a linear probe on STDP-learned features. The STDP test accuracy ($\NV{acc.stdp}\,\%$) therefore reflects STDP feature quality evaluated through a BP-trained classifier, not end-to-end STDP learning.

\subsection{RSA Pipeline}

\textbf{Feature extraction.} For each layer, activations were extracted on the 720 THINGS stimuli (resized to $224\times224$, standard ResNet input resolution). Convolutional activations were spatially averaged (global average pooling). All results are averaged across 5 random seeds per learning rule.

\textbf{RDM construction.} Model RDMs were computed as $1 - r$ (correlation distance).

\textbf{RSA score.} Model--brain alignment is Spearman's $\rho$ between the upper triangles of a model RDM and a subject RDM, restricted to the stimulus pairs defined below. The main convention ($\rho_{\text{subj}}$) correlates the model RDM with each subject's RDM and averages over the three subjects. The convention of earlier versions ($\rho_{\text{mean}}$) correlates the model RDM with the average of the three subject RDMs; it is reported alongside.

\textbf{Stimulus pairs (cross-run).} Each subject saw the 720 stimuli in ten runs, and sub-01 and sub-02 saw them in identical order. Responses within a run can share run-specific components (baseline drifts, run-wise noise), so for two subjects with the same order the dissimilarities of same-run pairs share a component that has nothing to do with the stimuli. This inflated the agreement between sub-01 and sub-02 (at LOC $\NV{agree.0102.LOC.all}$ on all pairs vs.\ $\NV{agree.0102.LOC.cr}$ on cross-run pairs, while sub-01 vs.\ sub-03 is $\NV{agree.0103.LOC.all}$ and $\NV{agree.0103.LOC.cr}$) and thereby the lower bound at LOC and IT (appendix, Table~\ref{tab:sens}). We therefore use only stimulus pairs that lie in different runs for all three subjects: $\NV{pairs.cr}$ of $\NV{pairs.total}$ pairs; $\NV{pairs.excl.pct}\,\%$ are excluded. Within a run, dissimilarity also depends on the distance between trials (Spearman correlation between dissimilarity and lag $\NV{lag.rho.min}$ to $\NV{lag.rho.max}$); at LOC and IT, neighbouring trials are on average anticorrelated (mean dissimilarity at lag 1 up to $\NV{lag.one.max}$). This would be consistent with collinearity between the overlapping hemodynamic responses of neighbouring trials in single-trial estimates, which is a further reason to exclude these pairs; we did not test this explanation. Excluding same-run pairs follows the same logic as run-wise cross-validation of dissimilarities, which keeps noise shared within a run out of the dissimilarity estimates \citep{walther2016}.

\textbf{Noise ceiling.} The lower bound follows \citet{nili2014}: each subject's RDM is correlated with the average RDM of the other two subjects, and the three correlations are averaged. It estimates how well a model that captures the structure shared across subjects would score, and is used here as a floor: a model below it leaves reliable shared structure unexplained. The upper bound (correlation of each subject with the average of all three, including itself) is not informative with three subjects: under a permutation null in which stimulus labels are shuffled independently per subject ($\NV{nperm}$ permutations), the upper bound stays large (V1: $\NV{ubnull.cr.V1.mean}$ against an observed $\NV{ub.cr.V1}$), because each subject contributes a third of the average it is compared with. It is therefore not shown in the figures. Under the same null the lower bound is $\NV{lbnull.cr.V1.mean} \pm \NV{lbnull.cr.V1.sd}$ (V1), so the observed lower bounds are far from chance at every ROI.

\textbf{Uncertainty.} 95\% confidence intervals come from a stimulus bootstrap: $\NV{nboot}$ resamples of the 720 stimuli with replacement (seed $\NV{bootseed}$), shared by all conditions, ROIs and quantities. Within each resample, pairs of a stimulus with itself are dropped and the cross-run pair set is recomputed. Differences between conditions, and between a condition and the lower bound, are computed on the same resamples, so their intervals account for the correlation between them. We call a difference significant when its percentile interval excludes 0. The three statements the conclusions rest on are treated as primary comparisons: the untrained network vs.\ BP at V1 and V2, BP vs.\ the untrained network at LOC, and FA vs.\ each other condition at V1 ($\NV{nprimary}$ of the 40 pairwise comparisons). They were designated in this revision, not before the original analysis. All other pairwise comparisons are exploratory. No correction for multiple testing is applied in the main text; all 40 intervals are reported, with the primary ones marked (appendix, Table~\ref{tab:pairwise}), and Benjamini--Hochberg and Holm corrections of the primary family are given in the appendix (Table~\ref{tab:fdr}).

\subsection{Partial RSA}

To control for low-level pixel similarity, a pixel RDM is computed as the pairwise correlation distance between the flattened RGB images (resized to $224$\,px on the shorter edge and centre-cropped to $224\times224$, as for the networks). Partial Spearman $\rho$ is estimated by residualizing the ranks of the model and the brain RDM vectors on the ranks of the pixel RDM vector and correlating the residuals. The pixel RDM was rebuilt for this version from the definition in the code of earlier versions; applied to the earlier analysis it reproduces the stored values to within $\NV{pval.dev}$ (they were stored at four decimals). Partial $\rho$ is computed per seed and subject on cross-run pairs and averaged as $\rho_{\text{subj}}$, with the same stimulus bootstrap.

\textbf{Interpreting absolute RSA scores.} Absolute Spearman $\rho$ values in this study are low (e.g., $\rho < 0.10$ at V1). They are not comparable with the scores of encoding-model benchmarks: RSA and encoding models measure fundamentally different quantities: RSA evaluates the geometry of the representational space (pairwise dissimilarity structure), whereas encoding models predict individual voxel responses, which is a more permissive criterion that can exploit stimulus-specific variance. RSA scores are further attenuated by the spatial resolution of fMRI relative to electrophysiology, and by our relatively small CNN architecture. The data themselves are of limited reliability: the lower bound is $\lb{V1}$ $\lbci{V1}$ at V1, $\lb{V2}$ at V2, $\lb{LOC}$ at LOC and $\lb{IT}$ at IT. Taken as a floor, it separates the conditions (model $-$ lower bound, with 95\% CI). At V1, only the untrained network reaches it ($\ml{rnd}{V1}$ $\mlci{rnd}{V1}$); every trained rule lies below it. At V2, the untrained network ($\ml{rnd}{V2}$ $\mlci{rnd}{V2}$) and STDP ($\ml{stdp}{V2}$ $\mlci{stdp}{V2}$) are not distinguishable from it, BP lies just below it (its interval ends less than $10^{-4}$ below zero), and FA and PC lie below it. At LOC and IT every condition lies below it. Reaching the lower bound is a weak criterion at V1: a single luminance value per image reaches $\rho_{\text{subj}} = \NV{lumv1.ps.cr}$ against the same V1 data, as much as the untrained network ($\rs{rnd}{V1}$; \newid).

Table~\ref{tab:rsa} provides the full numerical results. Several key patterns emerge.

\textbf{V1/V2---Untrained exceeds BP at 224\,px evaluation.} In early visual cortex, the untrained random-weights baseline \emph{exceeds} BP at V1 ($\rho = \rs{rnd}{V1}$ vs.\ $\rho = \rs{bp}{V1}$; $\Delta\rho = \dps{rnd}{bp}{V1}$ $\dpsci{rnd}{bp}{V1}$) and at V2 ($\rs{rnd}{V2}$ vs.\ $\rs{bp}{V2}$; $\Delta\rho = \dps{rnd}{bp}{V2}$ $\dpsci{rnd}{bp}{V2}$). The order at V1 is Random $>$ STDP $>$ BP $>$ PC $>$ FA, and all ten pairwise differences at V1 are significant in the per-subject convention; in the mean-RDM convention BP and STDP do not differ ($\dmrciF{bp}{stdp}{V1}$), and at V2 they do not differ in either convention. At this evaluation resolution, the convolutional architecture---local connectivity, ReLU, and pooling---is thus the primary driver of early visual alignment. When the models are evaluated at their $32$\,px training resolution, BP and the untrained network do not differ at V1 ($\rho_{\text{subj}} = \NV{res.bp.32}$ vs.\ $\NV{res.rnd.32}$; $\Delta\rho = \NV{res.gap.32}$, 95\% CI $[\NV{res.gap.32.lo},\NV{res.gap.32.hi}]$; cross-run pairs, model set of the resolution study, same architecture and training, 5 seeds); the untrained network's advantage at $224$\,px arises from the difference between training and evaluation resolution (\newid).

\textbf{LOC---Learning effect without differences among rules.} At LOC, BP ($\rs{bp}{LOC}$), PC ($\rs{pc}{LOC}$) and STDP ($\rs{stdp}{LOC}$) exceed the random baseline ($\rs{rnd}{LOC}$; BP $-$ Random $\dps{bp}{rnd}{LOC}$ $\dpsci{bp}{rnd}{LOC}$, PC $-$ Random $\dps{pc}{rnd}{LOC}$ $\dpsci{pc}{rnd}{LOC}$, STDP $-$ Random $\dps{stdp}{rnd}{LOC}$ $\dpsci{stdp}{rnd}{LOC}$). FA is borderline: its difference from Random is $\NV{d.ps.cr.bn.fa.rnd.LOC.4}$ $\dpsciF{fa}{rnd}{LOC}$ in the per-subject convention and excludes 0 in the mean-RDM convention $\dmrci{fa}{rnd}{LOC}$. No difference between two trained rules is significant (e.g.\ BP $-$ FA $\dps{bp}{fa}{LOC}$ $\dpsci{bp}{fa}{LOC}$).

\textbf{IT---Not distinguishable at low reliability.} At IT, the five conditions range from $\NV{range.ps.IT.min}$ to $\NV{range.ps.IT.max}$, and none of the ten pairwise differences is significant, including Random vs.\ BP ($\dps{rnd}{bp}{IT}$ $\dpsci{rnd}{bp}{IT}$). Reliability at IT is low, second lowest after LOC (lower bound $\lb{IT}$ $\lbci{IT}$), and every condition lies below this bound. The conditions are therefore not distinguishable at the reliability available at IT; this does not show that they are equivalent.

\textbf{FA lowest at V1.} FA produces the lowest brain alignment at V1 ($\rs{fa}{V1}$), and its differences from every other condition at V1 are significant in both conventions, suggesting that random feedback filters disrupt the representational structure that the convolutional architecture provides for free. This holds despite FA reaching $\NV{acc.fa}\,\%$ test accuracy---substantially above chance---indicating that FA learns task-relevant features that do not correspond to human visual representations. At V2, the ordering among FA, BP and PC depends on the layer assignment (Conv1 vs.\ Conv2; Appendix Table~\ref{tab:v2conv2}), whereas Random $>$ BP holds under both.

\section{Discussion}

\textbf{Architecture as the primary driver of early alignment.} Our most striking finding is that an untrained CNN \emph{exceeds} backpropagation at V1 ($\rho = \rs{rnd}{V1}$ vs.\ $\rs{bp}{V1}$, $\Delta\rho = \dps{rnd}{bp}{V1}$ $\dpsci{rnd}{bp}{V1}$). This goes beyond the observation of \citet{saxe2011} that random networks have non-trivial visual structure: in our setup, at $224$\,px evaluation, the convolutional inductive biases (local connectivity, ReLU, pooling) are not merely comparable to BP but produce higher early visual alignment. The advantage is not evidence that training degrades early-layer structure: when the models are evaluated at their $32$\,px training resolution, BP and the untrained network do not differ at V1 ($\rho_{\text{subj}} = \NV{res.bp.32}$ vs.\ $\NV{res.rnd.32}$; $\Delta\rho = \NV{res.gap.32}$, 95\% CI $[\NV{res.gap.32.lo},\NV{res.gap.32.hi}]$, model set of the resolution study), and the untrained network's lead at $224$\,px arises from the difference between training and evaluation resolution (\newid). Whether the same holds at larger scales (e.g., ResNet-50 on ImageNet) remains to be tested.

\textbf{On the untrained baseline.} The untrained condition's batch-normalisation layers hold their initialised statistics, so in evaluation mode they act as the identity, whereas every trained condition carries statistics accumulated during training. A $2\times2$ calibration control varying the calibration set and the calibration resolution, with the convolutional weights held bit-identical, indicates that this does not produce the result: with statistics calibrated at the evaluation resolution the untrained network still exceeds backpropagation at V1 by $\NV{cal.B}$ (CIFAR-10 statistics) or $\NV{cal.D}$ (statistics from a disjoint pool of THINGS images), $\NV{cal.B.n}$ and $\NV{cal.D.n}$ seeds (original analysis: all pairs, mean-RDM convention). The result is, however, strongly dependent on the evaluation resolution, which this paper holds fixed at $224$\,px; see \newid.

\textbf{Few reliable differences at higher areas.} At LOC, BP, PC and STDP exceed the random baseline, FA is borderline, and the differences among trained rules are small and not significant. At IT, no two conditions can be distinguished. This may reflect a genuine property of abstract categorical representations, but is also consistent with a capacity limitation: our small CNN trained on 8,000 CIFAR-10 samples may simply lack the representational power to differentiate at higher layers. Prior work with larger models suggests that BP-trained networks have a systematic IT advantage \citep{schrimpf2020}; whether this advantage reflects the learning rule or the additional model capacity remains an open question. The low reliability of the IT data (lower bound $\lb{IT}$, with every condition below it) also leaves little room for differences to be resolved with three subjects; true differences may exist but are not resolvable here.

\textbf{FA lowest at V1.} FA produces the lowest alignment at V1, significantly below all other conditions, despite reaching $\NV{acc.fa}\,\%$ test accuracy. This is consistent with random feedback filters disrupting the representational structure that convolutional architecture provides, though we cannot rule out that this reflects an optimization artifact specific to FA in convolutional networks rather than a fundamental incompatibility. After pixel control, FA's V2 score is $\NV{pr.partial.fa.V2}$ $[\NV{pr.partial.fa.V2.lo},\NV{pr.partial.fa.V2.hi}]$; it was already not distinguishable from zero without pixel control ($\NV{pr.std.fa.V2}$ $[\NV{pr.std.fa.V2.lo},\NV{pr.std.fa.V2.hi}]$). This result may be specific to CNNs, where spatial structure in feature maps interacts poorly with random feedback filters \citep{lillicrap2016}.

\textbf{Dissociation between task performance and brain alignment.} Task accuracy and brain alignment are dissociated in our small-scale setting. At V1, the untrained baseline ($\NV{acc.rnd}\,\%$ test accuracy) exceeds BP ($\NV{acc.bp}\,\%$), and at IT, conditions with test accuracies between $\NV{acc.rnd}\,\%$ and $\NV{acc.bp}\,\%$ cannot be distinguished. This suggests that, at least for small CNNs trained on CIFAR-10, the relevant axis is not ``how well does it classify'' but ``what representational structure does the objective induce at each layer.'' Whether this dissociation persists at ImageNet scale, where task-optimized models achieve substantially higher Brain-Score alignment \citep{schrimpf2020}, remains an open question.

\textbf{Implications.} Our results carry two concrete methodological messages. For computational neuroscience: studies comparing trained models to brain data must always include an untrained baseline with the same architecture. Without this control, architecture effects are confounded with learning effects---our data show this confound can be essentially complete at V1. The same control must be run at the training resolution: the untrained network's lead over BP at V1 is present at $224$\,px evaluation but absent at the $32$\,px training resolution; it arises from the mismatch between training and evaluation resolution, not from training per se degrading early-layer structure (\newid). A second message comes from the revision itself: when subjects share a presentation order, same-run stimulus pairs can inflate between-subject agreement and the noise ceiling, and should be excluded or modelled.

\subsection{Limitations}

Several limitations bound our conclusions. (1)~All models were trained on CIFAR-10 using a small custom CNN, far below the scale at which ResNet-50 or ViT models achieve competitive Brain-Score results. The small architecture limits representational capacity and may contribute to the absence of differences at IT. (2)~We have only 3 subjects, with sub-03 showing consistently weak signal; our core findings are driven by sub-01 and sub-02. With three subjects only the lower noise bound is informative. Replication on a larger dataset (e.g., Natural Scenes Dataset, $N = 8$) would strengthen generalizability. (3)~STDP was applied to static images via Poisson spike trains, discarding the temporal dynamics that STDP was designed to exploit. (4)~The best-layer-per-ROI analysis reveals that FC1 sometimes produces higher V1 alignment than Conv1, complicating the anatomical mapping assumption. (5)~Gabor-peakedness scores show no significant differences across learning rules, but this metric captures only a coarse proxy for V1-like filter structure. (6)~Restricting the analysis to cross-run pairs removes $\NV{pairs.excl.pct}\,\%$ of the stimulus pairs; it removes the shared run component, but the sensitivity analyses (appendix) show that the main conclusions do not depend on this choice. (7)~We use a single dataset (THINGS-fMRI); cross-dataset replication would strengthen all claims. (8)~Models were trained at $32\times32$ resolution but evaluated at $224\times224$; the size of the untrained baseline's V1 advantage depends strongly on the evaluation resolution, which is quantified in \newid.

\section{Conclusion}

We presented a systematic RSA comparison of four learning rules plus an untrained baseline at $224\times224$ resolution, averaged across 5 seeds, against the THINGS-fMRI benchmark. In our small-scale setup, the learning rule has little effect on cortical alignment: architecture dominates early visual representations (V1/V2), where the untrained baseline exceeds all trained rules and, at V1, is the only condition that reaches the lower noise bound. This advantage holds at the $224$\,px evaluation resolution; in the model set of the resolution study (same architecture and training, 5 seeds) the V1 gap is $\Delta\rho = \NV{res.gap.224}$ (95\% CI $[\NV{res.gap.224.lo},\NV{res.gap.224.hi}]$) at $224$\,px, and when the models are evaluated at their $32$\,px training resolution it vanishes ($\Delta\rho = \NV{res.gap.32}$, 95\% CI $[\NV{res.gap.32.lo},\NV{res.gap.32.hi}]$): BP and the untrained network do not differ at V1, and the untrained network's lead at $224$\,px arises from the difference between training and evaluation resolution (\newid). At LOC, three of the four trained rules exceed the untrained baseline (FA borderline) without differing from each other; at IT, no two conditions can be distinguished at the low reliability of the data---though this may also reflect model capacity limitations. FA consistently produces the lowest alignment at V1 despite above-chance accuracy ($\NV{acc.fa}\,\%$). These results suggest that, at least at this scale, learning-rule comparisons at early areas are dominated by the architecture and by the relation between training and evaluation resolution, and should report both. Whether these findings generalize to larger architectures trained on richer datasets remains an important open question.
